Acid rain was one of the biggest environmental problems in the news when I was a child. But today, I rarely hear about it. This chart explains why.One of the main chemicals behind acid rain is sulfur dioxide (SO₂), a toxic gas released into the air when fuels that contain sulfur — such as coal — are burned. This gas makes rain more acidic, damaging forests and freshwater ecosystems. It also contributes to air pollution that’s harmful when we breathe it in.The chart shows that the United States and the European Union have both reduced sulfur dioxide emissions massively. Relative to their peaks, emissions have fallen by around 94%.This has been achieved by deploying “scrubbers” in power plant chimneys (to capture SO₂ before it’s released), using coal with less sulfur in it, and burning less coal overall.Globally, emissions have also fallen, but they are still rising in some countries such as India and Indonesia.My colleague Hannah Ritchie wrote an article on air pollution, where she tells the story of acid rain and what the rest of the world can learn from it.
