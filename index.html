The Ice Giants' Magnetic Fields are Very Strange Compared to Earth's

Uranus and Neptune, the Solar System's ice giants, have interiors unlike the other planets. As a result, they also have magnetic fields unlike the others. They're messy and tilted, and Voyager 2, the only spacecraft to even flyby them, showed us how messy they are. New research uses models to try to understand what's going on with the pair of planets and their weird fields, and found that it's likely they change on very short timescales. They could already be much different than when Voyager 2 visited in the late 1980s.

In 2015, countries worldwide signed the Paris Agreement, aiming to keep the global temperature rise “well below 2°C” and to limit it to 1.5°C.To meet these targets, there are limits to the amount of carbon dioxide (CO2) that can be emitted. These are called carbon budgets. Every year we emit more CO2, these budgets shrink.In the chart, you can see estimates for how much CO2 the world can emit while staying below different levels of warming. This is based on a 50% likelihood of staying below it; if we wanted to guarantee we wouldn’t exceed these temperatures, our budget would be much smaller.For perspective, we’ve compared each budget with the projected amount of CO2 that the world is expected to emit in 2026. This tells us how many years we have left if emissions stay at their current levels.At current emission rates, the 1.5°C budget would run out around 2030. It seems implausible that global emissions will fall quickly enough to avoid this.The 2°C budget would last until mid-century. By taking action on climate change, we buy ourselves more time and can avoid this level of warming.This is based on the latest estimates from the Global Carbon Project.
